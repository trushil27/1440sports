The Case

Honeywell is splitting into three public companies, and the final step - spinning off Honeywell Aerospace as a standalone business - distributes on 29 June 2026. The new company will have more than $17B in annual revenue and be one of the largest publicly-traded aerospace suppliers, with leading positions in propulsion, cockpit and navigation systems, and auxiliary power. Two things make it worth a conversation. First, a spin-off is a brand-establishment moment: a newly independent public company must build an identity distinct from its former parent, which is exactly when first-mover brand investments get approved. Second, aerospace precision-engineering carries a genuine F1 halo - propulsion, materials and systems engineering are the sport's own language. The honest counterweight: this is a large, established B2B/B2G supplier whose customers (airlines, OEMs, defence) are won through procurement, not paddock hospitality, so its appetite for a motorsport sponsorship is unproven. Treat this as a relationship-and-halo play to open now, not a deal to force.

Why Now

The 29 June spin-off creates a finite brand-establishment window - new CEO Jim Currier and a fresh standalone leadership team will set the independent company's identity and brand-investment agenda in its first two quarters. Opening a conversation around the listing positions 1440 early, before the new entity's priorities harden, even though this is a longer-horizon relationship than a pre-IPO growth brand.

Why Aston Martin

Aston Martin is the fit: premium British engineering gravitas and a boardroom, government-adjacent hospitality audience that suits an aerospace brand's enterprise and institutional buyers, positioning Honeywell Aerospace as a precision-engineering halo rather than a mass-market sponsor. The aerospace lane is distinct from Aston Martin's existing technology partners, and as a US-headquartered company an American-team alternative (Cadillac) is the documented fallback if a US-sovereignty narrative is preferred.

Value to Aston Martin  — MODE B (tech serves the team operation)

MODE B. The value is a precision-engineering brand association, an R&D/innovation narrative (materials, simulation, sensors and systems engineering are shared language between aerospace and F1), and premium hospitality - not an in-car technical integration. Aston Martin gains a blue-chip aerospace partner and an engineering-credibility story; Honeywell Aerospace gains a global stage to establish its standalone brand.

Deal Architecture

Official Aerospace / Engineering Partner tier on Aston Martin (Cadillac as a US-narrative alternative), minimum three years, estimated $3-6M/yr, structured as an engineering-and-brand halo with a defined innovation/R&D narrative; calibrate to a relationship-first entry given unproven sponsorship appetite.

Risks & Counters

Unproven sponsorship appetite  Established aerospace suppliers sell via procurement, not paddock hospitality, and may see F1 as off-strategy. Counter: Lead with the brand-establishment moment, an engineering/innovation narrative and talent attraction - kept relationship-first and modest, not a hard sell; this is the rare window when a spin-off is open to defining a new identity.
Defence/B2G adjacency  Honeywell Aerospace has defence and government business, which can complicate consumer-facing brand placement. Counter: Anchor strictly to commercial-aerospace precision engineering and innovation, the brand-safe core, and keep activation enterprise/credibility-focused.
